The morning in the valley began early, and nobody was surprised.
A neighbor came out into the yard, looked at the sky and said: “It will rain”.
“Are you sure?” asked Anna.
“Almost,” he answered. “The swallows are flying low.”
“Then I will take an umbrella.”
“Take two—you will lose one of them anyway.”
She laughed and walked down the path toward the river.
The river is wide at this point, and the bank is gentle.
The boat was where they had left it yesterday.
An oar lay beside it in the grass and looked warm from the sun.
“Where are you going?” someone shouted from the hill.
Anna turned around, but she did not see anyone.
Far away a bell rang, and the herd moved toward the village.
The shepherd walked behind them, unhurried, with a long stick on his shoulder.
He knew every cow by name and did not like it when they were mixed up.
The village was small: about 40 houses and one school.
The school stood at the very edge, next to the forest.
It had 120 pupils from three neighboring villages.
The principal, Mr. Hale, had worked there since 1998.
Lessons started at 8:30 and ended at 2:15 p.m.
The break between the second and third lessons lasted 20 minutes.
The canteen sold tea, buns and apples.
A bun cost $2, an apple — $1.
Children mostly took apples because they came from the school orchard.
The orchard was planted in 1975–1976, and many trees bore fruit only ten years later.
The older pupils helped with the harvest.
In the evening they baked pies, and the smell reached the river.
Anna had loved that smell since childhood.
She remembered how her grandmother used to say: “Bread loves silence”.
Back then the radio stayed off until dinner.
Now her grandmother is gone, but the house stands in the same place.
In summer her nephew, a geographer, lives there.
He arrives on June 21 and leaves on September 3.
In that time he manages to walk the whole area with a map and a compass.
His map is made of paper because there is no phone signal in the woods.
The map shows streams, bridges, the ruins of a mill and an old dam.
The dam is 7 miles from the house, and he goes on foot.
The walk takes 2 hrs. if he does not stop.
Along the way he writes down everything he sees in a notebook.
The notes are detailed, but they are almost impossible to read…
His handwriting is such that he can barely make it out himself.
Once he lost the notebook and then found it in the boat.
The pages had gotten wet, and some of the notes were gone.
“Never mind,” he said, “I will write it again”.
A week later he returned to the dam and checked everything.
It turned out that the flood had destroyed the bridge in the meantime.
A new bridge was built by autumn, and trucks now drive across it.
They carry grain to the elevator, which is 35 miles away.
The grain is harvested in August, when the heat reaches +30 degrees.
In winter it can drop to −25, and then the river freezes to the bottom.
The ice holds a person, but it is better not to walk on it alone.
Fishermen go out anyway, sit by their holes and wait for a bite.
The fish bite rarely, but they are large.
A pike of 11 lbs. is considered an ordinary catch there.
The meat is dry, so it used to be pickled and fried.
The recipe was passed down from generation to generation, and nobody wrote it down.
But Anna did write it down once: 2 spoons of salt, 1 lemon, garlic, dill.
It turned out tasty, though not quite like her grandmother’s.
By evening the sky clouded over, just as the neighbor had promised.
The first drops fell on the roof around 6:00 p.m.
It rained all night, and by morning the river had risen by 30 cm.
The boat had to be pulled higher onto the bank.
Anna helped the neighbor, and together they finished in an hour.
“Thank you,” he said. “I would not have made it without you.”
“Don’t mention it. I forgot the umbrella at home anyway.”
“You see, so you would have lost one of the two.”
They laughed and went to drink tea.
The tea was strong, with mint and honey.
The neighbor got the honey from a beekeeper who lived across the river.
The beekeeper kept 15 hives and knew more about bees than anyone around.
He said a bee is smarter than many people, and it was hard to argue with him.
In July he collected the first honey, in August—the second.
A liter cost $12, and a line formed from the morning.
The honey was sold in town, too, where it was brought by bus.
The bus runs twice a day: at 7:40 and at 4:20.
The trip to town takes about 1.5 hrs.
Anna rarely went to town, only on business.
This time she needed to visit the archive on Garden Street.
The archive is open 9:00–17:00 and closed on Saturdays.
It kept birth records, building plans and old photographs.
She was looking for a picture of the school taken in the 1950s.
An archive employee, Ms. J. R. Cooper, found it within half an hour.
“You are lucky,” she said. “Nobody has opened that box in years.”
The picture turned out to be sharp: rows of children, a teacher, a flag.
On the back someone had written in pencil: “First graduation”.
Anna ordered a copy and paid for it on the spot.
The copy cost $5, and she had to wait a week.
When she returned, she showed the picture to the principal.
Mr. Hale looked at it for a long time and said nothing.
“That is my mother,” he said at last. “This one, in the second row.”
“And who is next to her?”
“I don’t know. Maybe a friend.”
The picture was hung in the school corridor under glass.
Now everyone who comes into the building stops in front of it.
Some recognize the faces, others simply look.
Next to it on the wall hangs the bell schedule.
It is printed in small type, but it is easy to read.
At the end of the year the school holds a fair, and parents bring baked goods.
Last time they raised $1200 and spent $950.
The rest went to buy books for the library.
The librarian, Mary Stone, made a list of 80 titles.
Fairy tales and encyclopedias came first on the list.
Then came reference books, atlases and dictionaries.
The books arrived in boxes, and the children unpacked them eagerly.
Every book was stamped and entered into the catalog.
The catalog was kept by hand in a large notebook.
Later it was moved to a computer, but the notebook was not thrown away.
It lies in a cabinet next to the lesson plans.
The cabinet is old, the door creaks and the lock has not worked for ages.
The principal promised to order a new one before the end of the year.
The money will come from the county budget if everything goes as planned.
The plan was approved at the meeting on March 14.
Nine people out of 11 attended the meeting.
Two were absent because of illness, and their votes were counted in writing.
The result of the vote: 8 for, 1 against, 0 abstained.
The caretaker voted against, and one can understand him.
New cabinets have to go somewhere, and there is little room in the classrooms.
They decided to clear the corner room where the skis used to be.
The skis were moved to the shed, and they are kept there now.
In winter they are taken out, waxed and handed out in gym class.
The ski track runs along the river, through the forest and back to the school.
The loop is 3 km long, and the children finish it in 25 min.
The best results come from those who live farthest from school.
They walk every day, and their stamina is good.
In spring the skis are put away, and the exam preparation begins.
Graduates take their exams in the district center.
They are taken there by bus, and their parents wait at the entrance.
The results are announced at the end of June at the general assembly.
This year 18 graduates out of 20 entered universities.
Two decided to work first and study later.
The principal did not object: “Experience never hurt anyone”.
Summer comes to the village again, and everything repeats.
Combines go out to the fields again, bells ring again.
Anna opens the window and listens to the river.
She knows tomorrow will be hot and does not take an umbrella.
What happens next—nobody knows.
And what if it rains again?
Then we will drink tea with honey again.
So be it.
